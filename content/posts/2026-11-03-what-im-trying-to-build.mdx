I can explain why each of my projects is worth doing. Explaining why I'm doing all of them takes longer.

There's software, a food project, a house in Hungary, time spent getting people into the water. I like having different things to work on. They ask different things of me, and I don't want every part of my life to happen through a screen.

They also make things possible for each other. A software business can help pay for something physical. Making a physical product forces questions about materials and costs that are easy to leave vague in an idea. People you meet through one project can become the people you build the next one with.

I want more of those connections. If something works, I'd like the benefit to reach beyond that one thing. We should come away better able to do something else, with a bit more experience, perhaps some money to invest, and people we know how to work with.

That starts with making something useful. Someone has a job to get done, and what we've made helps them do it. Or they buy something we've put real care into, enjoy it, and come back. I like being able to point to that. I like knowing that something exists because we took the trouble to make it.

Doing it together changes the work. Someone sees a problem I missed, or has a better idea, and suddenly the thing becomes more interesting than what I had in mind. I'd like there to be enough room for that, including the part where I have to let go of my own version.

There's a personal reason for it too. I want to feel useful and understood. I like having my effort appreciated, and I want the people working with me to feel that theirs is appreciated as well. A project that pays people properly and leaves them keen to work together again has given the next idea a much better chance.

I'd like these things to sustain themselves. Customers, income, enough money to maintain what we've made. A business that needs a fresh burst of enthusiasm from me every week would eventually become a fairly exhausting job. I want some of the things I start to become strong enough to carry on without my constant attention.

But I can't make them permanent. Even a successful business can disappear. Software gets replaced. People move on. Knowing that doesn't make me less interested in whether something works. It makes me less willing to put off enjoying life until it does.

If a project takes five years, those five years are part of my life already. They're part of the lives of everyone working on it. I'd like us to enjoy enough of those ordinary days that we don't need a successful ending to justify all of them. There will still be difficult weeks and work nobody particularly wants to do. I want us to care about what it's like to be involved while we're doing it.

The flowers in Daniel Keyes' [*Flowers for Algernon*](https://danielkeyesauthor.com/algernon.html) make sense to me here. An experiment gives Charlie Gordon extraordinary intelligence, following an earlier experiment on a mouse. The gains don't last. At the end, Charlie [asks for someone to put flowers on Algernon's grave](https://teachers.americanliterature.com/author/daniel-keyes/short-story/flowers-for-algernon). The flowers will wilt as well. He asks for them anyway.

I can want something to last and still care about it knowing it won't. The time spent making it, and what we become able to do together, don't disappear from our lives when the thing itself ends.

When I have the next idea, I'd like to be able to pick up the phone and call the same people.
