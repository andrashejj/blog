I want to build things people use, with people I like.

That explains quite a lot of what I do. Writing software, working on a house, making granola, getting people together in the water. Put them on a list and it looks like I can't pick a subject. I understand that. Sometimes I probably should pick one and finish it before starting something else.

But I can see the connection. Someone is trying to understand where their money is going. Someone needs to organise a job without spending the evening chasing messages. A kid wants to catch a wave. Friends need somewhere to sit and eat. I like having something to contribute to those situations. I like working out what would help, making it, and finding out whether it does.

The finding out is a big part of it. You can spend weeks pleased with an idea, then someone uses it for five minutes and shows you what you missed. Or they get it immediately, and you get that ridiculous rush of having made something that makes sense to another person.

I want more of that. Being understood through something you've made feels good.

The work crosses over, too. Years of building software make me want to test a food idea with people before making a thousand bags. Making something physical brings me back to costs and materials when I've spent too long moving things around on a screen. And getting people together gives me a reason to build things I wouldn't have thought of sitting at my desk.

I also want these things to sustain themselves. A product should earn enough to pay the people making it. A group should be able to meet without one person arranging everything forever. A house should be somewhere we can live and spend time together, with maintenance we can manage. I'd like the things I start to become less dependent on me being there to push them along.

That's quite a lot of ordinary work. Costs, repairs, another conversation about who does what. I enjoy the beginning of things much more easily than I enjoy all of that. But if I want something to keep going, I have to care about the less interesting parts too.

And even then, it won't last forever.

Software gets replaced. A business can work for years and then stop working. Children grow up and find other people to surf with. The house will need fixing again. Even the group of people you thought would always be around changes.

I know this, and I still catch myself wanting to build something permanent. Something I could eventually look at and say, there, that one's done. It can keep going now.

Daniel Keyes' [*Flowers for Algernon*](https://danielkeyesauthor.com/algernon.html) belongs somewhere in this. Charlie Gordon undergoes an experiment that increases his intelligence, following an earlier experiment on a mouse called Algernon. The gains don't last. Algernon declines and dies, and Charlie faces his own decline. At the end, [he asks for someone to put flowers on Algernon's grave](https://www.sparknotes.com/lit/algernon/summary/).

What I take from the book is how much Charlie wants to belong. He wants to learn and be taken seriously. Becoming more capable changes his life, but it doesn't settle his need to be loved or make his relationships easy. His worth was there before the experiment, and it remains as he loses what it gave him.

I recognise the wish to feel useful. I want to add something. I want someone to appreciate the effort, and I want to feel they understand what I was trying to do. It's easy for me to put a lot of that into work. Making something gives me a way to reach people, especially when I can explain the thing better than I can explain myself.

But I'd like to feel I have a place with them on a day when I haven't made anything useful, too. And I'd like the people around me to feel that from me.

The flowers give all of this an image I can hold in my head. Someone takes the trouble to bring them to a small grave. The flowers will wilt as well. Charlie asks for them anyway. To me, there's affection in that gesture, and an acceptance that you can care for something you can't keep.

I can put that image beside the things I'm working on without pretending they're all going to succeed. I want to give them a proper chance. If a product works, the people using it get something useful for as long as it works. If we spend a few years making something together, we've spent those years learning how to work with each other. That time happened, even if we eventually close the company or go and do something else.

Knowing things end makes me care more about what it's like to be involved while they're happening. I'd hate to build a successful business that made everyone miserable, including me. Or organise so much around surfing that I stopped enjoying being in the water. I can get very focused on making the next thing work and forget that other people are having their lives alongside mine.

The connections I want take time. You keep showing up, do your share, learn what someone else is good at. After a while you can make things together that you wouldn't have thought of alone. You also get to know when they're tired, or when they'd rather talk about something other than the work.

I want enough room for that. For someone to change the idea, take over a part of it, or tell me I'm making it too complicated. For us to enjoy a day together while there are still plenty of things left to fix.

The house will have unfinished jobs when people come to stay. I'd like to put food on the table and sit down with them anyway.
